UK Manufacturing & Industry ESG — the carbon-price world

UK ETS, CBAM, CCAs/CCL, industrial decarbonisation — and why it's a different game from CRE. Where CRE runs on an asset-value logic anchored on MEES, manufacturing runs on a carbon-price logic: a direct cost per tonne, a border price, an energy tax, and a customer/Scope-3 squeeze from above. The buyer, the data, and the "why now" are all different.

01

The mental model — four pressures, a different centre of gravity

In CRE the centre of gravity is MEES, the great leveller — a building rating. Manufacturing has its own stack, and its centre of gravity is a price on carbon. Hold these four pressures:

Pressure 1 · dominant

Carbon pricing — the direct cost per tonne

UK ETS (cap-and-trade on installations) · UK CBAM (border price on imports, from 2027) · CCL / CPS (tax on business energy and on power-generation fuels).

Pressure 2

Energy & efficiency obligations

ESOS (4-yearly audit: buildings + transport + process) · CCAs (efficiency targets that buy down the CCL) · SECR (energy/carbon in the accounts — shared with all large firms).

Pressure 3

Customer / value-chain — your emissions are someone's Scope 3

PPN 006 (public contracts → Carbon Reduction Plan) · OEM scorecards (supply data or be delisted) · SBTi / CDP (the frameworks the demand is expressed in).

Pressure 4 · shared with all sectors

Cross-cutting disclosure

UK SRS (ISSB S1/S2) · TCFD (legacy, absorbed into UK SRS). The only layer CRE and manufacturing fully share.

The crucial difference from CRE: Pressures 1 and 3 barely exist for a landlord — a building owner pays no direct carbon price and faces no border tax. Conversely, MEES has no factory equivalent — there is no "you cannot operate this plant below an efficiency rating" leasing ban. Only the disclosure layer is fully shared — which is exactly why the venture's "single source of truth" thesis can travel across sectors.

02

Why manufacturing is the battleground — the emissions reality

UK total net territorial emissions in 2024 were ~373 MtCO₂e Confirmed · DESNZ final, Feb 2026, down ~53% on 1990. Against that, manufacturing is the single largest-emitting industrial SIC section — roughly 62 MtCO₂e in 2023, ~16% of all UK emissions Verify (it has been the top section since 2019). Industry is decarbonising in lumpy, structural ways: 2024 industrial emissions fell ~7%, largely via blast-furnace closures in iron & steel and lower coal Verify — i.e. partly by losing capacity, not only by efficiency. That is the politically charged backdrop (jobs, competitiveness, carbon leakage) the whole policy stack is trying to manage.

The strategic read: manufacturing is hard-to-abate. A chunk of its emissions are process emissions (the CO₂ from making cement from limestone, or steel from iron ore) that efficiency cannot remove — they need CCS, hydrogen or electrification. That is why the mix is a price (ETS/CBAM/CCL) plus a state capital programme, rather than a simple performance standard. Where CRE can largely be fixed with retrofit, industry needs a price signal and infrastructure.

03

The UK ETS — the core carbon price

What it is. A cap-and-trade scheme launched in 2021 (the post-Brexit replacement for EU ETS participation), run by the UK ETS Authority. It puts an absolute, declining cap on in-scope installations and lets them trade allowances (UKAs). It covers energy-intensive industry, power generation, and domestic aviation — installations above activity thresholds, not whole companies.

The cap (the teeth). Reset in 2022 to align with net zero; the 2026 cap is 77.4 MtCO₂e Verify and declines annually. Within it, an industry cap (free allocation) was set at 40% of the total cap from 2024, reducing each year Verify.

Free allocation — the part that's changing fast. The second allocation period now starts in 2027 (2026 is a bridge year), re-timed to align with CBAM Confirmed. For CBAM-covered sectors, free allocation begins to phase out from 2027 over an indicative ~9-year trajectory Verify; other sectors continue in reduced form to 2030. The November 2025 review confirmed current benchmarks for 2027, moving to updated EU benchmarks for 2028–2030 Verify. The translation: emitting carbon is moving from "mostly free" to "increasingly a direct cost" across 2027–2030. That is the demand event.

Scope is expanding. Domestic maritime enters from July 2026; energy-from-waste / incineration reaches full inclusion in 2028 (MRV-only from 2026); Phase II is confirmed 2031–2040; an ultra-small / low-emitter route exists 2026–2030 Verify.

Price. Volatile and policy-sensitive — roughly £30–£55/tCO₂e through 2025 Verify. Quote the direction (rising, converging toward the EU price if linkage completes), never a spot price.

The EU–UK linkage. On 19 May 2025 the UK and EU agreed a "Common Understanding" to work toward linking the two ETSs; the EU Council backed a negotiating mandate on 12 Nov 2025, and formal negotiations were announced to begin January 2026 Verify. A link would mean mutual recognition of allowances, cross-system trading, and — critically — mutual CBAM exemptions, which the UK estimates could save exporters ~£800m by 2030 Verify. In motion, not done.

Why it matters for the venture The ETS itself is a trading/compliance regime where licensed verifiers and brokers already operate — not a software gap to occupy. But the free-allocation phase-out + benchmark tightening + scope expansion means a growing population must produce audit-grade, benchmark-aligned emissions data annually and forecast their allowance exposure to plan capex. That data-assembly-and-forecasting layer beneath ETS compliance is the analog of the CRE "MRV inventory engine." Don't build the trading desk or become the verifier — sit alongside the gatekeeper.
04

UK CBAM — the border price (new, from 2027)

What it is. A Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism that prices emissions embedded in imported goods, so importers face a charge comparable to a UK producer's ETS/CPS cost. Legislated in the Finance Bill 2025–26 (with preparatory provisions in Finance Act 2025), it starts 1 January 2027 Confirmed.

Scope. Imports of aluminium, cement, fertiliser, hydrogen, and iron & steel Confirmed. Glass and ceramics are excluded from launch (deferred); electricity is not included. Indirect (electricity-related) emissions are excluded until 2029 at the earliest Confirmed · Nov 2025 update.

Who's liable and how. The charge falls on the UK importer, who must register with HMRC once they meet a £50,000 threshold of CBAM goods over a rolling 12 months Confirmed (raised from £10,000, so only larger importers are caught). Connected companies can register as a group. The first accounting period is all of 2027, with the return and payment due by 31 May 2028, moving to quarterly from 2028 Verify. Expected revenue ~£140m (2027–28) rising to ~£180m (2028–29) Verify.

The ripple beyond importers. Though the legal duty is the importer's, the practical effect is a supply-chain data demand: overseas producers will be asked for verified embedded-emissions data per consignment (or the importer pays punitive default values). CBAM turns "embedded carbon of this batch of steel" into a per-shipment number someone must produce, evidence and defend.

Interplay with the ETS. CBAM and the ETS free-allocation phase-out are deliberately coupled — as free allocation falls for CBAM sectors from 2027, the border price rises to replace it as the anti-leakage tool. Two halves of one mechanism.

Why it matters for the venture CBAM is an embedded-carbon accounting and evidence problem at the product/consignment level — structured data that software solves, and where generic corporate carbon tools are weak. But the £50k threshold means it's a mid/large-importer problem, not a universal one — treat it as a high-value vertical wedge, not a mass market.
05

CCL + CCAs — the energy tax and the deal that buys it down

Climate Change Levy (CCL). A tax on business energy use (electricity, gas, LPG, solid fuels), paid by industrial/commercial/agricultural/public-sector consumers and shown as a line on the bill. HMRC collects it; DESNZ owns the policy. Main rates are uprated annually (next rise 1 April 2026); the gas-vs-electricity rebalancing completed in 2024 Verify. A parallel Carbon Price Support rate applies to fossil fuels used in power generation.

Climate Change Agreements (CCAs). Voluntary sector agreements, administered by the Environment Agency, in which energy-intensive operators commit to efficiency/carbon targets for a large CCL discount — up to roughly 90%+ on electricity and ~65–89% on gas Verify · confirm current %. Missing two-yearly targets triggers buyout payments (usually far smaller than the savings).

The new scheme is live. DESNZ confirmed a new CCA scheme on 16 Oct 2024, running to 31 March 2033, with the first new target period (TP7) starting 1 January 2026 Confirmed. New applications open 1 January–31 August each year, 2026–2030; eligibility covers 50+ energy-intensive sectors; ~8,400 UK facilities currently hold a CCA Verify.

Why it matters for the venture A CCA is a recurring measure-target-report relationship with two-yearly reporting and live consequences. The under-served job is connecting CCA target tracking → ESOS findings → ETS/CBAM exposure → disclosure into one workflow instead of four disconnected spreadsheets.
06

ESOS for industry — the 4-yearly audit (shared with CRE, but wider)

What it is. A mandatory energy audit every four years for large UK undertakings (≥250 employees, or ≥£44m turnover with ≥£38m balance sheet). It explicitly spans buildings, transport and industrial processes — so for a manufacturer it reaches the production line, not just the offices.

Phase 4 status. Compliance period 6 Dec 2023 → deadline 5 Dec 2027; qualification date 31 Dec 2026. Mandatory net-zero assessment was pushed to Phase 5 (2027–2031); in Phase 4 it's a voluntary option (PAS 51215-1/-2:2025). Action-plan + annual public progress reporting carry over from the Phase 3 reform. Display Energy Certificates and Green Deal Assessments are removed as compliance routes. Administered by the Environment Agency (+ devolved).

The weakness = the opportunity ESOS has historically been audit-and-ignore: an assessor reports cost-effective savings, the company files it, and the recommendations die in a PDF. There is still no hard duty to act — only to audit and (now) report progress. Connecting ESOS findings → an actual decarbonisation pathway → ETS/CCA/disclosure outputs is a workflow nobody has cleanly built — and it spans both CRE and industry, the cross-sector bridge the expansion thesis needs.
07

Industrial decarbonisation — the state capital programme

Because much of industry is hard-to-abate, the UK pairs the price signals with a state-funded infrastructure programme — the "how do you physically abate" layer.

  • Industrial Decarbonisation Strategy (2021) set the direction: CCUS, hydrogen, fuel-switching, electrification, efficiency for industrial clusters.
  • CCUS cluster sequencing: Track-1 — HyNet (NW England + N Wales) and the East Coast Cluster (Teesside + Humber), selected Oct 2021. Track-2 — Acorn (Scotland) and Viking (Humber). HyNet + ECC target ~33 MtCO₂/yr capture and ~31,000 jobs Verify.
  • Funding: ~£21.7bn over 25 years committed (Oct 2024, up from £20bn in March 2023), with ~£3.9bn across 2025–26 Verify. First clusters now expected operational ~2028 (≈2 years behind schedule); competitive allocation for CCS projects from 2027 Verify.
  • Adjacent support: the Energy Intensive Industries Compensation Scheme (relief on electricity policy costs), hydrogen production support, electrification grants.
Why it matters for the venture This is capital-intensive delivery owned by incumbents (CCUS developers, energy majors, EPC contractors) — not the venture's lane. But it generates demand for the data the venture can own: a plant negotiating cluster connection, an EII claim, or a hydrogen-switching business case all need credible, audit-grade emissions data. Feed the delivery players; don't replace them.
08

The Scope-3 / supply-chain demand mechanism — the real multiplier

This is the most important section for an expansion thesis, because it is the demand engine that links CRE, manufacturing and logistics into one market. The mechanism: large organisations are obliged (or choose) to report and cut Scope 3, whose dominant component is purchased goods and services — so they push the obligation down onto suppliers, who must measure and disclose regardless of their own size.

1 · Public procurement — PPN 006. Since September 2021, bids for UK public contracts worth ≥£5m/year must publish a Carbon Reduction Plan — Scope 1, 2 and a defined set of Scope 3, plus a commitment to net zero by 2050 Confirmed. Non-compliance is an automatic disqualification. With public spending ~45% of GDP — and Tier-1 suppliers pushing the requirement down their own chains — it cascades far below the £5m line.

2 · Private OEM pressure. Major buyers — industry reporting names the likes of Rolls-Royce, JLR, BAE, Siemens, Unilever Verify · single secondary source — run supplier sustainability scorecards and increasingly delist suppliers who can't provide emissions data. The expression layer is SBTi and CDP; the calculation rests on the GHG Protocol Scope 3 Standard and DEFRA conversion factors.

Why this is the keystone It converts ESG from a large-company obligation into a whole-supply-chain one. A 30-person fabrication shop with no ETS installation, no UK SRS duty and no MEES building is still told, "give us your product carbon footprint or lose the contract." That SME long tail — forced to measure by a customer, not a regulator — is structurally the same wedge as the CRE long tail forced by MEES: broad, under-served, must-act, too small for the Big Four. The largest cross-sector market for a productised measurement + reduction-plan tool sits here.

10

Venture opportunity spaces (manufacturing)

Mapping the pains to wedges:

  • The industrial MRV / emissions-inventory engine — audit-grade, benchmark-aligned Scope 1/2 for ETS installations and CCA holders, with the forecasting to plan allowance exposure.
  • Embedded-carbon / product-footprint calculation for CBAM and customer demand — per-product/per-consignment carbon, evidenced and exportable into CBAM returns and CDP/customer scorecards.
  • The Scope-3 / supplier-data platform for the SME long tail — the highest-volume opportunity; cross-sector by nature and the clearest bridge from CRE outward.
  • ESOS-to-action workflow — turning the four-yearly audit that "dies in a PDF" into a live reduction pathway feeding ETS/CCA/disclosure. Spans CRE + industry.
  • Reduction-plan tooling — the recurring "now what" after measurement, consistent with the CRE wedge.

What NOT to build first:

  • The carbon-trading desk or anything that tries to become the ETS verifier — licensed gatekeepers; sit alongside.
  • The CCUS / abatement delivery layer — capital-intensive, incumbent-owned.
  • Anything assuming a MEES-style universal mandate for factories — it doesn't exist. Build for the price-and-customer-driven data need, not a non-existent asset-rating ban.
11

Honest caveats

  • This is the fastest-moving area in the whole programme. The ETS reform, CBAM detail, the new CCA scheme, the ESOS Phase 4/5 split, and the EU-UK linkage all moved within the last 6–18 months, several still in consultation or negotiation.
  • Carbon prices are not figures to quote — quote direction (rising, converging on EU), not a spot UKA price.
  • CBAM is mid-build — draft regulations and HMRC guidance were still being finalised in early 2026; default values and return cadence may shift.
  • The OEM-scorecard naming is single-sourced — treat the company list as illustrative, not authoritative.
  • Thresholds are simplified here — ESOS, CCA eligibility and CBAM group rules all have edge cases; confirm against the regulator for any specific buyer.
