UK Supply Chain / Scope 3 — the demand engine

Not a sector — a cascade. A large organisation with a disclosure obligation or a net-zero target must account for emissions it doesn't own, the bulk of which sit with its suppliers — so it pushes the obligation down the chain, and each supplier pushes it down again. A firm that escapes every direct regulation is forced to measure anyway, by its customer. That is the single largest, most cross-sector demand pool the venture can address.

01

The mental model — 15 categories, one cascade

Scope 3 is indirect value-chain emissions — everything not in Scope 1 (own combustion) or Scope 2 (purchased energy). The GHG Protocol Corporate Value Chain Standard defines 15 categories, split upstream (1–8) and downstream (9–15):

Upstream · 1–8
  1. Purchased goods & services ◄ biggest
  2. Capital goods (CRE)
  3. Fuel & energy (not S1/S2)
  4. Upstream transport & distribution (logistics)
  5. Waste
  6. Business travel
  7. Employee commuting
  8. Upstream leased assets (CRE)
Downstream · 9–15
  1. Downstream transport & distribution
  2. Processing of sold products
  3. Use of sold products ◄ often biggest for OEMs
  4. End-of-life of sold products
  5. Downstream leased assets (CRE)
  6. Franchises
  7. Investments (finance)

Two facts make this the centre of gravity: Scope 3 is typically 70–90% of a company's total footprint Verify · SBTi/GHG Protocol — most of the problem lives outside a firm's own walls; and each prior sector plugs straight into a category — a manufacturer's output is its customer's Category 1, a logistics operator's freight is Categories 4 and 9, a landlord's tenant energy is Categories 8/13. Every sector's "output carbon" is somebody else's Scope 3 input — the unifying insight of the whole expansion programme.

02

Why Scope 3 is the hardest and the biggest

Scope 1 and 2 are yours — you have the meters and the fuel bills. Scope 3 is data you don't control, sitting in hundreds or thousands of third parties. The measurement-quality ladder runs:

  1. Supplier-specific (primary) — the supplier's own verified emissions, a product carbon footprint (PCF) from an LCA, or an EPD. Most accurate.
  2. Activity-based — actual quantities (kg, km, kWh) × emission factors.
  3. Average-data — industry-average factors per unit.
  4. Spend-based (secondary) — £ spent × an emission factor (Exiobase, DEFRA). Least precise; a valid start, not a destination.
The core opportunity Almost everyone starts spend-based (it needs only the purchase ledger) and is pushed toward supplier-specific data by their own targets and their customers' demands. Closing that gap — getting primary data out of suppliers — is the core operational problem of Scope 3, and the core software opportunity.
03

The demand channels — who forces it (and a key UK nuance)

Unlike CRE (MEES) or industry (ETS), Scope 3 in the UK is driven more by markets than by a hard regulatory deadline — a nuance that matters for product design.

  • UK disclosure regulation is a weak Scope 3 driver. SECR is Scope 1 + 2 only. UK SRS (ISSB S2) requires Scope 3 in principle but carries a transitional/indefinite relief in the UK adaptation. So the statutory push is softer than the EU's.
  • EU CSRD / ESRS E1 does mandate Scope 3 (with a primary-vs-secondary data-split disclosure) — but the 2026 Omnibus cut CSRD scope to the very largest firms, concentrating UK spillover in big cross-border groups.

The strong UK drivers are market and procurement:

  • Public procurement — PPN 006: bids for contracts ≥£5m/yr require a Carbon Reduction Plan covering Scope 1, 2 and relevant Scope 3, with non-compliance an automatic disqualification. The state is the single biggest buyer, cascading the requirement down its suppliers.
  • Private buyer demand + SBTi supplier-engagement targets: SBTi requires near-term Scope 3 targets covering ≥67% of total Scope 3 Verify, a materiality screen of all 15 categories (exclusions ~5%), and supplier-engagement targets under which a company commits to getting suppliers to set their own science-based targets within ~5 years Verify — the cascade written into the standard itself.
  • CDP and EcoVadis questionnaires — the channels through which large buyers actually collect the data, scored and benchmarked.
  • CBAM embedded carbon (manufacturing) — a regulatory-adjacent Scope-3 driver for imported goods.
  • Construction embodied carbon (CRE link) — UKGBC roadmap, EPD-led procurement, the proposed Part Z; Scope 3 can be 80–95% of a built-environment org's footprint Verify · UKGBC.
The UK takeaway Because the push is market-led rather than deadline-led, the demand is continuous and relationship-based (a customer keeps asking, every year, in their format) rather than a one-off compliance scramble. That suits a recurring software product better than a deadline-driven one.
04

The cascade — why this is an economy-wide market

The multiplier

One large buyer with an SBTi target or a PPN 006 obligation must report its Category 1 emissions → it asks its hundreds of suppliers for data → each supplier, to answer credibly (and keep the contract), must build its own inventory → and each of those suppliers then asks its suppliers.

So a single obligation at the top propagates down through tiers to the SME long tail — firms with no direct regulatory duty of their own. They measure because a customer demands it, on pain of being delisted or bid-blocked — exactly the dynamic seen in the manufacturing (OEM scorecards) and logistics ("bid-blocked diesel fleet") modules. The addressable population is therefore not "large UK companies" but "anyone in a supply chain that touches a large company or a public contract" — which is most of the economy.

The great-leveller analog This is the structural analog of MEES as the great leveller in CRE: MEES forces every landlord regardless of size; customer Scope-3 demand forces every supplier regardless of size. Same shape, far wider footprint.

06

The data-collection problem — where the software lives

The whole of Scope 3 reduces operationally to a few hard, repeatable jobs that are exactly what software does well:

  • Supplier data collection at scale — surveys/portals to pull primary data (or PCFs/EPDs) from the top suppliers, with spend-based estimation as the fallback for the long tail. (Rule of thumb: moving the top 10–20 suppliers from spend-based to primary data is a 1–2 cycle effort.)
  • Footprinting across all 15 categories with the right factors (DEFRA, Exiobase) and a defensible primary/secondary split.
  • Carbon Reduction Plan / disclosure generation in the formats buyers actually ask for (PPN 006 template, CDP, EcoVadis, customer questionnaires).
  • "Answer once, reuse everywhere" — the single-source-of-truth thesis at value-chain scale: a supplier completes its inventory once and exports it into every customer's questionnaire.
07

Venture opportunity spaces — the expansion keystone

  • The cross-sector supplier/footprint platform for the SME long tail — the highest-volume opportunity in the whole programme: help the firms forced by customer/PPN demand build a credible footprint and Carbon Reduction Plan, and respond to questionnaires once. This makes the venture multi-sector by default, because the long tail spans manufacturing, logistics, CRE supply and services.
  • Product carbon footprint (PCF/EPD) tooling — primary-data generation for manufacturers feeding Cat 1 and CBAM.
  • Per-shipment carbon (logistics) and whole-life/embodied carbon (CRE) as category-specific feeds into the same engine.
  • Supplier-engagement-as-a-service for large buyers — the other side of the cascade: helping a big organisation collect from its suppliers and run its SBTi supplier-engagement target. Also a powerful distribution channel — land one large buyer, reach its whole supply base.

What NOT to build:

  • Become the SBTi validator / CDP scorer — independent gatekeepers; feed them, don't replace them.
  • The deep-science LCA consultancy at the bespoke end — partner with LCA specialists.
  • Anything assuming a hard UK Scope-3 mandate — the UK push is market-led, so sell on winning/keeping contracts and answering customers, not a statutory deadline.
08

Honest caveats

  1. SBTi is mid-revision — the V2 Corporate Net-Zero Standard was in draft in late 2025; thresholds (the 67% coverage, supplier-engagement mechanics, FLAG rules) may shift Verify.
  2. UK Scope-3 regulation is deliberately light — don't over-claim a statutory driver; the real force is procurement + customers + voluntary targets. A strength for a recurring product, a weakness for a deadline pitch.
  3. CSRD spillover is narrower than older sources say — post-Omnibus, only the largest cross-border firms.
  4. Data quality is contested — spend-based numbers are easy but weak; the value is in the supplier-primary-data uplift, which is hard and where buyers focus scrutiny.

What you should now be able to do

  • Explain why Scope 3 is 70–90% of a typical footprint and name the 15 categories (upstream 1–8, downstream 9–15), with Cat 1 usually largest.
  • Explain the data-quality ladder (supplier-specific → activity → average → spend-based) and why the value is in moving up it.
  • State the key UK nuance: SECR is Scope 1+2 only and UK SRS has Scope 3 relief, so UK Scope-3 demand is market-led (PPN 006, OEM scorecards, SBTi, CDP), not deadline-led.
  • Explain the cascade and why it pulls the SME long tail into measurement — the great-leveller analog to MEES.
  • Map how manufacturing, logistics and CRE each become a Scope-3 category for someone else.
  • Identify the cross-sector supplier-data platform as the expansion keystone, and what to avoid building.
