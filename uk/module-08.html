Module 8 is different in kind: it's not "here's what exists," it's "here's how to decide." Per your brief, this is built as a decision framework you and boss apply yourselves — optimised for fastest path to first revenue, showing both the consulting-led and product-led paths with honest tradeoffs. It hands you a tool for thinking, not an answer to accept. Reading time: ~30 minutes. This is the one to read slowly, twice.

0. Why a framework, not a recommendation

I could tell you "build X." It would be a mistake, for three reasons:

  1. The best decision depends on things only you two know — your real runway, your risk appetite, who's actually in your network, what work you'll still be excited about in eighteen months when it's hard.
  2. A recommendation is a snapshot; a framework is reusable. You're going to learn a lot in the next six months that none of us know now. A framework lets you re-run the decision as facts change. A recommendation goes stale the moment a buyer says something surprising.
  3. You're the research/strategy founder. Owning the reasoning, not borrowing the conclusion, is literally your job. This module is meant to make you dangerous in a room with boss, not dependent on me.

So what follows is: the candidate wedges, the scoring criteria, a scoring method you run yourselves, the stress-tests to apply to whatever wins, the two go-to-market paths compared, and a sequencing logic. Your judgment is the missing input throughout. I'll flag exactly where.

3. How to score (the method)

Don't overthink the precision — the discipline of scoring matters more than decimal-point accuracy. For each wedge, score each criterion 1–5 (1 = poor, 5 = excellent for that criterion). Then multiply by the weight and sum.

Critical instruction on scoring honestly:

  • For C3 (domain barrier) and C4 (reachability), a high score means low barrier / easy to reach — i.e. score it 5 when it's good for you, not when the barrier is high. Keep the polarity consistent: 5 always = good for the venture.
  • Score what's true, not what you hope. The framework is only as good as your honesty. If you don't know a value, that's a signal you need to go find out — which is itself valuable output.

4. The two go-to-market paths, compared honestly

Per your brief, here are both paths laid out straight, with no thumb on the scale.

Path A: Consulting-led (services first, productise later)

The motion: Sell UKSRS-readiness / GRESB / MEES-exposure as a service. Do the work manually (with tooling you build internally). Learn the workflow intimately. Productise the repeatable parts over 12–24 months.

ProsCons
Fastest cash (your #1 priority) — bill within weeks of first clientServices revenue is linear, not scalable; you trade time for money
Builds trust + relationships you otherwise lack as unknownsRisk of becoming "just a consultancy" forever — the classic trap
Teaches you the real workflow → de-risks the eventual productGross margins lower than software; investors value it less
Low capital requirement — fits bootstrapFounder time gets consumed by delivery, not product-building
The proven path (EVORA, Deepki, Carbon Intelligence all did it)Hard to context-switch from service mindset to product mindset later

Best if: runway is tight, you need revenue fast, you value learning the domain hands-on, and you have the discipline to deliberately carve out product-building time rather than drowning in delivery.

Path B: Product-led (build first, sell the tool)

The motion: Pick one sharp problem (e.g. MEES exposure, or Scope 3 data capture), build a focused product, sell it as software from the start.

ProsCons
Scalable from day one — software margins, venture-grade upsideSlowest to first revenue — months of building before anyone pays
Plays directly to your tech/data edgeYou're guessing at the workflow without having lived it (domain-gap risk)
Cleaner story for future investorsCold-selling software as an unknown India-based vendor is hard (Module 6)
Forces focus on a repeatable, defensible thingHigher chance of building the wrong thing — no client teaching you

Best if: you have enough runway to survive a long build, high conviction about a specific pain, and a way to get early design-partner feedback so you're not building blind.

The honest synthesis on the two paths

Given your stated fastest-revenue priority, Path A scores higher on the criterion you said matters most. But notice the trap the framework reveals: Path A optimises C1 (time-to-revenue) at some cost to C6 (defensibility) and long-term scalability. If you only optimise for fast revenue, you can end up a healthy consultancy that never becomes a product company — which is a fine business, but a different one than "ESG/compliance tech company" implies.

The reconciling move (worth considering, not prescribing): Path A as the engine, Path B as the destination, with an explicit tripwire. Enter consulting-led for cash and learning — but set a pre-agreed trigger ("when we've done N engagements" or "when we've seen the same workflow M times") at which you force the shift to productising. The discipline is deciding the tripwire now, in cold blood, before delivery revenue becomes comfortable and the product ambition quietly dies. Many services-to-product transitions fail not because the strategy was wrong but because the tripwire was never set.

5. The stress-tests (apply these to whatever wins)

Whatever wedge tops your scoring, run it through these five tests before committing. A wedge that survives all five is robust. One that fails two or more needs rethinking.

Test 1 — The "why us, why now" test. Can you finish these two sentences convincingly? "We specifically are the right team to do this because ___." "Now is the right time because ___." If the answers are generic ("we're smart and hardworking," "ESG is hot"), the wedge isn't sharp enough. For UKSRS-timing wedges, "why now" is strong (four-month-old regulation). For "why us," your honest answer today leans on tech/data skill + cost base — which is real but not unique. Strengthen it.

Test 2 — The "first ten customers" test. Can you name — not describe, name — ten plausible first customers, and a route to reach each? If you can't name ten, you don't yet understand the market well enough to commit. This test alone will tell you whether C4 (reachability) is real or wishful.

Test 3 — The "consultant displacement" test. For your chosen wedge, what does the buyer do today instead? If the answer is "pay JLL/EVORA to handle it," you're displacing a trusted incumbent — you need a reason they'd switch (cheaper? faster? more focused?). If the answer is "nothing / struggle with spreadsheets," you're creating a new behaviour — which is harder than it sounds.

Test 4 — The "data access" test. Does the wedge require data you can actually get? The Scope 3 wedge (W3) is painful precisely because the data is hard to extract from tenants — that's the moat and the risk. Be honest about whether you can access the inputs your wedge needs. A brilliant tool with no data is nothing.

Test 5 — The "boss alignment" test. Does the wedge fit what boss actually wants to build and is good at? He came from automotive software, identified this via brainstorming, not lived pain. A wedge that needs deep relationship-selling into UK property might fit neither of you. A data/tooling wedge might fit the team better. This is a real conversation to have explicitly, not assume.

6. How to re-run the framework when your priority changes

You told me to optimise for fastest revenue. But priorities shift — runway changes, a co-founder joins, an investor appears. Here's how to re-weight:

If your priority becomes…Re-weight like this
Fastest revenue (current)C1 ×3, C4/C5 ×2 — favours services + acute-pain wedges
Largest long-term ventureC6 (defensibility) ×3, C2 (tech fit) ×3, C7 ×2 — favours product + data-moat wedges
Lowest risk given gapsC3 (domain barrier) ×3, C4 ×3 — favours wedges needing least domain depth + easiest access
Best tech/data fitC2 ×3, C6 ×2 — favours data-engine and platform wedges (W3, W5, W6)

Re-running the scoring under different weightings is one of the most useful things you and boss can do together — because watching which wedge wins under which priority reveals what you're actually optimising for. If you disagree on the answer, you've probably got an unspoken disagreement about priority. Surface it.

7. The sequencing logic (first 90 days — a structure, not a script)

Whatever you choose, the shape of a sensible first 90 days is similar. Not a prescriptive plan — a structure to adapt.

Phase 1 (≈weeks 1–4): Validate the wedge with real humans. The single highest-value action across every module's caveats was the same: talk to real UK CRE people. Before building or selling anything, get 5–10 conversations with sustainability leads, asset managers, or FDs. Test whether the pain you've hypothesised is real and urgent. This de-risks more than any amount of further desk research.

Phase 2 (≈weeks 4–8): Run the framework with real inputs. Re-score the wedges using what you learned in Phase 1 (not my outside guesses). Pick one. Write the "why us, why now" in one paragraph. Name your first ten targets.

Phase 3 (≈weeks 8–12): Land one paying engagement or design partner. Path A: sell one consulting engagement, even a small one. Path B: secure one design partner who'll co-build and give feedback (ideally paying, even nominally). Either way, the goal is one real customer relationship that turns hypotheses into evidence.

The principle underneath: every 90-day cycle should convert the biggest current unknown into knowledge. Right now your biggest unknown isn't regulatory (you've got that) — it's whether real buyers feel the pain acutely enough to pay. Aim the whole quarter at answering that.

8. What to deliberately NOT do (the anti-strategy)

Equally important as what to do. Based on everything in Modules 1–7:

  • Don't build "another portfolio ESG dashboard." That's the consolidation buzzsaw (Module 7). Deepki and Measurabl will out-feature and out-data you.
  • Don't try to compete with iSBEM / the SBEM-tool vendors. (The original briefs established this — small, mature, specialist market; wrong buyer.)
  • Don't chase tier-1 REITs first. They're well-served, slow to buy, and demand reference clients you don't have. The mid-market is the more realistic entry even if less glamorous.
  • Don't hardcode regulatory deadlines (MEES dates move — Module 4) or assume a fixed ruleset (CSRD got gutted, EPC methodology is shifting — Module 5). Build for adaptability.
  • Don't pitch on "scary fines." (MEES enforcement is weak — Module 4.) Pitch on lettability, value, refinancing, investor pressure.
  • Don't try both go-to-market paths at once. Pick A or B as primary. Hedging both means doing neither well.
  • Don't sell software cold from India before you have trust signals. (Module 6/7.) Earn trust via services, a UK domain hire, a design partner, or a channel relationship first.

9. The one-page version (for you and boss)

If you distil this whole module to a single shared artefact, it's this:

The decision: Which wedge (W1–W6), via which path (A: services-led / B: product-led)?

The priority lens (agreed): Fastest path to first revenue — but consciously, not at the accidental expense of ever becoming a product company.

The method: Score each wedge against C1–C8 (weighted for fast revenue), honestly, with real inputs from buyer conversations — not desk guesses.

The tension to hold: Fastest revenue (services) ≠ best long-term asset (product). If you go services-first, set the productisation tripwire now.

The gating unknown: Do real UK CRE buyers feel a specific pain acutely enough to pay now? Everything hinges on this. Go find out before committing.

The anti-strategy: No generic dashboard. No iSBEM competition. No tier-1-first. No cold software sales from India without trust. No hedging both GTM paths.

10. What you should now be able to do

After this module, you should be able to:

  • Run the wedge-scoring framework yourself, with your own honest inputs, and re-run it under different priority weightings
  • Articulate the central tension (fastest revenue vs. best long-term asset) and hold it consciously rather than resolving it by accident
  • Compare the consulting-led and product-led paths on their real tradeoffs
  • Apply the five stress-tests to any wedge — especially "name ten customers" and "why us, why now"
  • Explain why validating buyer pain with real conversations is the gating next step
  • State clearly what the venture should deliberately not do, and why each anti-pattern is a trap

11. Honest caveats (the most important ones in the whole program)

1. This framework organises judgment; it doesn't replace it. The scores are inputs you generate, not truths I've handed you. Garbage in, garbage out. Its value is forcing explicit, honest, comparable reasoning — not producing a "right answer."

2. Every module feeding this has been desk research. I'll say it one final time because it's the single biggest risk: we have built a sophisticated understanding of this market without having spoken to anyone in it. That's a strong foundation and an incomplete one. The framework's output is only as good as the buyer-reality inputs you feed it — and right now those inputs are hypotheses. Phase 1 (talk to real people) isn't optional; it's the difference between strategy and speculation.

3. The fastest-revenue optimisation has a known failure mode. Optimising hard for quick cash can quietly trap you in services and starve the product ambition. I've built in the "tripwire" idea to counter this, but no framework can enforce founder discipline. That's on you and boss.

4. Your team's domain gap remains the central risk. Everything here assumes you close it — via these modules, a domain hire, an advisor, or design partners. A tech-strong team that stays domain-light will struggle to out-execute incumbents with deep property expertise. The knowledge base mitigates this; it doesn't eliminate it.

5. I may be wrong about the seams. "No UKSRS-for-CRE tool exists yet" is absence-of-evidence, not proof. Someone may be building exactly this in stealth. Validate before you bet.

12. Closing the program — where this leaves you

You started eight modules ago with zero domain knowledge. You now have:

  • The world — how UK CRE works, who owns and operates buildings, how money flows (M1–2)
  • The rules — UKSRS, MEES, and the full regulatory/standards stack, current as of mid-2026 (M3–5)
  • The buyers — who pays, who champions, who blocks, and what they buy today (M6)
  • The competition — who's already there, the consolidation trend, and the five seams (M7)
  • A decision framework — to choose your wedge and path, re-runnable as you learn (M8)

That is a genuine working foundation for the conceptualisation phase. It's enough to let you hold your own with boss, ask sharp questions of any advisor or potential hire, and reason about the venture rather than guess.

The honest final word: the modules took you as far as thinking can go. The next gains come from contact with reality — real buyer conversations, a real first engagement, real feedback. The framework in this module is designed precisely to absorb that reality and turn it into decisions. Use it as a living tool, not a finished document.

When you're ready, sensible next steps could be: (a) a buyer-interview guide for those Phase 1 conversations, (b) a one-page venture thesis to align with boss, or (c) a deeper drill on whichever wedge your scoring favours. Any of those, when you want them.
