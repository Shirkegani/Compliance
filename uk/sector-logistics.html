UK Logistics & Transport ESG — the fuel-and-fleet world

Vehicle mandates, fuel obligations, warehousing, and the Scope-3 squeeze from clients. Logistics has no single defining regulator the way CRE has MEES or industry has the ETS; it sits at the intersection of three logics — a fleet-emissions (Scope 1) problem, a warehousing problem that is really CRE, and above all a customer/Scope-3 problem. The dominant "why now" is not a fine — it's being bid-blocked by a customer for running a diesel fleet.

01

The mental model — four pressures, plus a borrowed one from CRE

Logistics is a connective sector: its fleet is its own Scope 1, its warehouses are CRE, and its freight is its customers' Scope 3. That is why it can't be modelled as a self-contained industry — and why it's the natural bridge in a multi-sector expansion.

Pressure 1

Vehicle transition mandates — you must change what you drive

ZEV Mandate (annual ZEV sales targets for new cars & vans) · 2030 petrol/diesel ban (hybrids & ICE vans to 2035) · HGV phase-out (≤26t from 2035; all from 2040).

Pressure 2

Fuel obligations & prices — what you burn costs more

RTFO (renewable content on road fuel) · SAF Mandate (from 2025) · Red-diesel removal (2022) · UK ETS (domestic aviation now; maritime from July 2026).

Pressure 3

Operating levers — where you drive costs money

Clean Air Zones / ULEZ — daily charges on non-compliant vehicles.

Pressure 4 · dominant

Customer / Scope 3 — your freight is your client's Scope 3

3PL → shipper emissions reporting · PPN 006 + OEM scorecards (carbon data or lose the contract) · per-shipment / per-route carbon as a deliverable.

Borrowed from CRE

Warehousing

MEES / EPC / BREEAM apply to sheds, depots and (energy-hungry) cold storage — the existing CRE stack, re-used.

02

Why transport is the battleground

  • Domestic transport is the UK's single largest emitting sector — ~30% of all UK emissions in 2024 Confirmed · DESNZ, and it has fallen only ~15% since 1990 — far less than power or industry, because traffic growth offset efficiency gains.
  • It is hard to abate at the heavy end: cars and vans are electrifying fast, but HGVs, aviation and shipping lack mature, cheap zero-emission options — which is why the mix here is mandates + fuel obligations + carbon pricing rather than one performance standard.
  • The translation: transport holds the largest share of the remaining UK emissions problem, so it attracts the most regulation — and a logistics operator is exposed to several instruments at once.
03

The vehicle transition — a forced fleet-replacement cycle

The ZEV Mandate (in force since January 2024) sets annual zero-emission sales targets on vehicle manufacturers — rising from 22% of cars / 10% of vans in 2024 to ~80% of cars and 70% of vans by 2030, and 100% by 2035 Verify (the 2026 car target is ~33% Verify). Missing targets means buying credits or paying a per-vehicle penalty; 2025 reforms added flexibilities but kept the trajectory.

The phase-out dates Confirmed · gov.uk: no new petrol or diesel cars from 2030 (reinstated in 2024–25 after the previous government pushed it to 2035); hybrids and new ICE vans permitted until 2035 (small-volume makers exempt); HGVs — new non-zero-emission trucks ≤26t banned from 2035, all HGVs from 2040 (announced at COP26; DfT consulting on the detail as of early 2026).

What this means for a fleet operator. The mandate binds manufacturers, but it reshapes the operator's world: ICE supply tightens, residual values move, and replacement cycles must be planned around electrification and charging/depot infrastructure (~£6bn of private charging investment tied to 2030, plus EV/van grants) Verify. Existing diesels can keep running and be sold second-hand — so this is a capex-and-planning forcing function over a decade, not a cliff.

04

Fuels & fuel costs — the base is rising

  • RTFO (Renewable Transport Fuel Obligation). Obliges road-fuel suppliers to ensure a rising renewable share — ~14.6% by 2025 Verify — via blending, RTFCs or a buy-out, with double rewards for waste/residue feedstocks. Administered by the DfT; the cost flows through to diesel prices.
  • SAF Mandate (from 1 January 2025). Obliges aviation-fuel suppliers (≥15.9 TJ/yr) to blend sustainable aviation fuel — ~2% in 2025 → ~10% by 2030 → ~22% by 2040 Verify — with a HEFA cap (~92% in 2027 declining to ~35% by 2040), a power-to-liquid sub-obligation, a ≥40% GHG-saving test and a buy-out. SAF was removed from the RTFO into its own mandate. A structural cost-add to air cargo.
  • Red diesel removal (April 2022). Rebated ("red") diesel was withdrawn from most sectors — construction, manufacturing, logistics plant — leaving it mainly for agriculture and rail Verify. This raised the effective fuel cost of off-road plant and depot equipment overnight.
  • UK ETS. Already covers domestic aviation; domestic maritime enters from July 2026 Verify — a direct carbon price on coastal shipping, with international shipping prospectively later.

The pattern: the fuel cost base for moving goods is on a structural upward path, by design.

05

Operating levers — Clean Air Zones and ULEZ

Beyond carbon, local air-quality regimes impose daily charges on non-compliant vehicles entering city zones — Clean Air Zones in cities such as Birmingham, Bristol, Bradford, Sheffield, Tyneside and Portsmouth, and London's ULEZ/LEZ (ULEZ expanded to all London boroughs in 2023) Verify. For a fleet these are a direct, recurring operating cost and a further reason to electrify last-mile and urban vehicles. They target NOₓ/particulates rather than carbon, but reinforce the same fleet-transition pressure.

06

Warehousing — the CRE overlap (don't research it twice)

A logistics operator is also a commercial-property owner/occupier: big-box distribution sheds, last-mile depots, and — most energy-intensively — cold storage. So the entire CRE stack applies to the warehouse estate: MEES (the leasing-ban / stranded-asset logic), EPCs, BREEAM and green-lease dynamics. Cold storage in particular is a heavy electricity load, making it both a Scope 2 hotspot and a retrofit target.

Implication The venture's existing CRE tooling already covers the warehousing dimension of logistics — no new research needed there. Logistics is partly a re-use of CRE, not a fresh build.
07

Reporting that bites — the shared layer, where transport is the headline number

The cross-cutting disclosure/energy regimes apply exactly as elsewhere — and for many companies transport is the largest line in Scope 1:

  • SECR explicitly requires transport energy and emissions in the annual accounts.
  • ESOS explicitly spans transport alongside buildings and industrial process — so the four-yearly audit reaches the fleet.
  • UK SRS / TCFD sit above, consuming the same fleet/fuel data.

So a haulier or 3PL is already obliged to assemble fleet and fuel data for the shared layer — the same "gather once, report many" logic, with transport as a primary input.

08

The Scope-3 / customer squeeze — the dominant force

This is where logistics differs most from a standalone sector: its output is, by definition, somebody else's Scope 3. Freight movement is GHG Protocol Scope 3 transportation & distribution for every shipper that uses it — so the demand for logistics carbon data comes from customers, hard and early:

  • 3PLs and carriers (the DPDs, DHLs and hauliers) are asked by shippers for emissions data per client, per lane, increasingly per shipment — to feed the shipper's own disclosure.
  • PPN 006 (public contracts ≥£5m → Carbon Reduction Plan) and private OEM scorecards mean a logistics supplier with a diesel-only fleet can be "bid-blocked" from low-carbon-supply-chain contracts Verify.
  • The expression layer is again SBTi, CDP, and the GHG Protocol / DEFRA conversion factors.
The strategic point In logistics the customer is the regulator. A small haulier faces no ETS installation and no MEES building — but is told by its largest client, "report your emissions per route or lose the work." That converts per-shipment / per-route carbon into a sellable data product, and pulls the SME-haulier long tail into the market the same way MEES pulls the CRE long tail.

10

Venture opportunity spaces (logistics)

  • Fleet & fuel emissions tracking (Scope 1) — turning telematics, fuel cards and DEFRA factors into audit-grade fleet emissions feeding SECR/ESOS/UK SRS.
  • Per-shipment / per-route carbon as a client-reporting product — the 3PL → shipper data flow; the highest-value, logistics-specific wedge and a direct on-ramp into customers' Scope 3.
  • Warehousing energy & EPC/MEES — re-use of the CRE tooling for the shed/depot/cold-store estate (especially refrigeration-heavy cold storage).
  • ESOS-transport-to-action — connecting the four-yearly transport audit to an actual fleet-decarbonisation plan.
  • The SME-haulier long tail — self-serve measurement + Carbon Reduction Plan for the small operators forced by client Scope-3 demand.

What NOT to build:

  • The vehicles, charging infrastructure or fuels themselves (capital/physical, incumbent-owned).
  • Telematics hardware — commoditised; integrate with it, don't rebuild it.
  • Own the data and reporting layer; feed the physical players.
11

Honest caveats

  1. Phase-out dates are politically volatile — the car ban moved 2030→2035→2030 inside three years; HGV regulation detail is still in consultation.
  2. ZEV percentages and SAF targets quoted here are indicative trajectories Verify; exact annual figures and flexibilities change.
  3. Red diesel and CAZ details vary by sector and city; confirm for a specific operator.
  4. Maritime/aviation ETS scope is expanding and partly consultative (international shipping, SAF interactions).
